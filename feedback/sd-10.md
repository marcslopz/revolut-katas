## A. Assessment: PASS, strong

Your first mock outside fintech, and it transferred well. You **didn't over-apply fintech
patterns** (no residency theatre, no money-path machinery), and you applied what fits:

- per-title serialization with a conditional update;
- a durable waitlist;
- outbox + queue for notifications;
- every external-call outcome handled.

The waitlist and hotspot reasoning, which you self-raised, was the best Phase 1 work across all ten
mocks.

The persistent gap is the same as sd-6/sd-8/sd-9: **the schema misses fields your own flow uses**.
The pickup branch for waiters, the recipient for notifications, and `available_copies` for your own
locking SQL were all missing.

## B. Three strongest things

1. **Hotspot as contention, then waitlist, then table**: you spotted lock contention yourself, proposed
   a waitlist, and swapped the broker-queue idea for a table the moment the waitlist became long-lived.
   That's exactly the "decisions follow new facts" habit.
2. **Concurrency done right when it counted**: `UPDATE titles SET available_copies = available_copies −
   1 WHERE … AND available_copies > 0`, with the loser joining the waitlist in the same transaction.
   It solves correctness and the hotspot with one mechanism.
3. **Failure paths of the external call**: all outcomes covered without prompts, plus decoupling state
   transitions from notification delivery (after one probe).

## C. Three biggest risks for the real interview

1. **Schema completeness (4th mock)**: the drill hasn't been done yet. Before showing a table, walk
   your flow and tick every field. Here: "reserve at *my* branch → where's the branch?", "notify the
   member → where's the email?".
2. **Say the locking strategy when you design the flow**, not when asked. "In one transaction" is not
   a concurrency answer; "conditional update on the title row" is.
3. **Phase 4 needs a push every time** (sd-8, sd-9, sd-10), and the bottleneck should come with a
   number that proves it's the bottleneck.

## D. Architectural gaps or inconsistencies

- `titles.number_of_copies` in the schema vs `available_copies` in the locking SQL.
- A global per-title counter vs "available at the selected branch": per-branch availability needs a
  per-(title, branch) counter, or picking a copy with `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`.
- A worker labelled "outbox" but drawn publishing directly; "stop producing outbox messages" vs "keep
  writing the outbox, pause publishing".
- The Redis fail-fast path was proposed and then silently dropped.

## E. Missing non-functional considerations

- Staff desk availability (99.95%): what happens at the desk if the central system is unreachable
  (see K).
- Business SLIs for the pipeline: "ready" notifications delivered within X minutes; holds that expired
  without a delivered notification.
- Analytics (hot titles, longest waitlists) should run on a replica or warehouse, not the primary.

## F. Over/under-engineering

- **Right-sized**: one Postgres, a waitlist table, a scheduled worker, outbox + queue for notifications.
- **Briefly over**: Redis as the reservation authority for bestsellers (dropped).
- **Under**: the check-in transaction (should hand the copy straight to the first waiter).

## G. What a strong candidate might have covered

- **Check-in in one transaction**: `copy → reserved for waitlist head`, delete or mark the waiter
  fulfilled, outbox("ready" or "in transit"). A copy is never `available` while someone waits, so
  fairness is structural.
- The pickup window starts on **arrival scan** at the pickup branch, not when the copy is reserved.
- Waitlist rows: `(isbn, position, user_id, pickup_branch_id, status, created_at)`, UNIQUE(isbn,
  user_id), and index `(user_id)` for "my waitlists". Position shown = count of active rows ahead.
- Reserve pre-flow: count of the member's active items < 10 and no overdue loans (index
  `(reserved_by, status)`).

## I. A better architecture (post-mock, brief)

```
Reserve TX:  check limits (reserved_by index) → UPDATE title_branch_stock … WHERE available > 0
             → claim a copy (FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED) │ else INSERT waitlist(position, pickup_branch)
Check-in TX: copy returned → if waitlist head exists: assign to head (reserved / in_transit) + outbox
             else available_copies += 1
Arrival scan: in_transit → ready_for_pickup, pickup_due_at = now + 3 days, outbox(ready)
Worker:      timers only (expire hold → same "hand to next waiter" TX; reminders; lost at 30 days)
Relay:       outbox → queue → notification consumer → email/push (breaker, DLQ); state never blocked
```

## K. Real-world bonus (not scored)

- **Offline circulation**: library desks keep working when the central system is down (checkouts and
  returns queued locally, synced later). That's how real systems meet a high desk availability target.
- **Works vs editions**: members usually want *the book*, not a specific ISBN. Real waitlists are per
  "work" across editions/formats, which changes the waitlist key.
- **Hold shelf**: reserved books sit on a special shelf, and the 3-day window is counted in opening
  days, not calendar days.
- **Interlibrary loan / consortium**: transfers between library systems, a natural cross-cell case.
- **Reading-history privacy**: many libraries delete loan history at return unless the member opts in.
  A stricter version of the 2-year rule.

## Post-review note (coaching, 2026-10-05): the real 10x bottleneck

The notification providers were **not** the bottleneck: at 10x, ~5M notifications/day ≈ 60/s on
average, deferrable through the queue, which is easily within email/push provider capacity. DB writes at
10x ≈ 3.3k row writes/s, still one primary.

**The first thing to break is the bestseller title row.** ~140 reservations/s at 10x (far more in the
first minute of a release), all serialised on one `titles` row at ~5 ms per transaction:

- occupancy = arrival rate × lock time = 140/s × 0.005 s = 0.7 → 70% of every second the row is locked;
- in the first-minute burst (e.g. 50k in 60 s ≈ 830/s) it's 4.2 → over 100%: the queue of waiters grows
  without bound and requests time out.

Options and their costs:
1. Shorten the lock: only the counter update inside the locking transaction (small change, buys margin).
2. Per (title, branch) counters (spreads the lock across rows; more allocation logic).
3. Insert-only waitlist + one asynchronous allocator per title for releases (no contention, fair by
   insertion order; the user learns the outcome seconds later).

Expected answer: "writes ~3k/s fine, notifications ~60/s and deferrable; the first thing to break is the
single-row lock on a bestseller: 140/s × 5 ms = 70% occupancy, over 100% in the first minute."
