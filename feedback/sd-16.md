## A. Assessment: PASS (moderate)

The concurrency core and the counter design are the strongest you've done on stock. You applied the
lessons from the lite mocks without being asked:
- negative counter for sales that can't be refused
- conditional decrement for reservations
- lock ordering
- both interleavings traced

**Phase 4 opened by yourself for the second mock running.**

What keeps it at moderate:
- the drawn schema was missing the core table and fields
- a per-till key was used as a global key, the same miss as case 2
- the no-show/collection race ended with a counter error
- two over-engineered pieces (precomputed distances plus Redis, and two-region sync replication) that
  the numbers didn't call for

## B. Three strongest things

1. **Counters designed to commute.** Walk-in sales decrement unconditionally and can go negative,
   reservations decrement conditionally, and the pick-time cancel restores the counter. You proved
   it by tracing both interleavings with numbers.
2. **The right questions about the integration.** You found the till stream as the real write load
   (1.5M ≫ 40k), and you asked how a collection sale differs from a walk-in one, which prevents a
   double decrement.
3. **Phase 4 unprompted**: cells per region with residency, and a numbered bottleneck at 10x.

## C. Three biggest risks for the real interview

1. **Idempotency key scope**: `sale_id` "unique per till", like case 2's `order_id` "unique per
   marketplace". The key is the full natural identity: `(store_id, till_id, sale_id)`. Ask
   yourself "unique within what?" every time.
2. **Counters on secondary transitions**, the cross-case pattern from today. The main paths were
   right; the `no_show` → late collection sale was not. For every transition, say what happens to
   `quantity` and `available`.
3. **Sizing to the requirement you were given**: 99.9% became 99.99% and drove two regions;
   200 static stores drove a precomputed table plus a cache. Re-read the number before adding a
   component.

## D. Architectural gaps or inconsistencies

- The drawn tables have no `sku_stores(store_id, sku_id, quantity, available_quantity)`, although
  every workflow uses it. `orders` has no `store_id`, and `order_lines` has no `order_id`.
- "The system tells the cashier the reservation is no-show" contradicts your own decoupled till
  design and the stated fact that tills never depend on us.
- Two regions with synchronous replication vs the 99.9% target, and vs the <1 s reservation SLA:
  every commit pays the WAN round trip, and writes stop if the other region is unreachable, unless
  replication degrades to async.
- The first shard key (`sku_id`) was chosen for the read while the bottleneck was writes (fixed
  after a probe).

## E. Missing non-functional considerations

- **Till-event consumer lag and DLQ.** If the consumer stalls, availability is wrong for every
  store. That's the most important metric in this design.
- **Staff authorization**: staff can act only on their own store's orders.
- **Business metric**: pick-time failure rate, meaning reservations cancelled because a walk-in got
  there first. That's the direct signal for whether you need a buffer (see G).

## F. Over/under-engineering

- **Over**:
  - The `user_distances` precomputed table plus Redis. With 200 static stores, compute the
    distance from the request's location to all 200 in memory, or use PostGIS on `stores`.
  - Two-region synchronous replication for 99.9%. A multi-AZ hot standby in one region covers it.
- **Under (minor)**:
  - The upsert on the first delivery of a SKU to a store.
  - Batching till events per store before sharding.

## G. What a strong candidate might have covered

- **Hold time asked in Phase 1**: "how long do we keep reserved items, and what happens if the
  customer doesn't come?" It's the domain-specific implicit requirement of click and collect.
- **An online buffer per store**, the case 2 lesson. Don't show the last 1–2 units online, because
  walk-ins take them before staff pick. It trades a few online sales for fewer pick-time
  cancellations.
- **Late collection after `no_show`**: the order was released, so treat the sale as a regular sale
  (`quantity −= q, available −= q`) and mark the order `collected_late`. That needs a conditional
  transition from `no_show`, and lines that don't match are simply a regular sale.
- **Before sharding**: batch till events per store (one transaction per store per second instead of
  one per line), then shard by `store_id`, grouping nearby stores in the same shard so the
  availability view usually hits one shard.

## H. Topics to practise before the next mock

1. **"Unique within what?"** for every idempotency key that comes from an external system.
2. **Counter walk per transition** (cross-case pattern): for each state change, `quantity` and
   `available` before and after.
3. **Draw the core table first**: the entity that holds the invariant (here `sku_stores`) goes on
   the canvas before `users` or `skus`.
4. Keep doing what worked: actions first, volumes per actor, Phase 4 opener, tracing interleavings
   with numbers.

## I. A better architecture (post-mock, brief)

**Boxes:**
- the stock API behind a load balancer
- one Postgres primary with a multi-AZ hot standby
- a till-event consumer, with lag/DLQ alerts

**Data:**
- `sku_stores(store_id, sku_id, quantity, available_quantity)`, with PK `(store_id, sku_id)` and an
  index on `(sku_id, store_id)` for the view
- `reservations(id, store_id, customer_id, status created|picked|collected|no_show|cancelled,
  picked_at, ...)` plus `reservation_lines(reservation_id, sku_id, qty)`
- `till_sales(store_id, till_id, sale_id)` with UNIQUE on all three, and `reservation_id` nullable

**Flows:**
- **View:** compute the nearest 10 of 200 stores in memory, then one query by `sku_id` and
  `store_id IN (...)`, showing `available − buffer`.
- **Reserve:** lock rows in `sku_id` order, then conditionally decrement `available` (all or
  nothing).
- **Walk-in sale:** `quantity −=` and `available −=`, unconditionally.
- **Collection sale:** if the reservation is `picked`, only `quantity −=`. If it's `no_show`, both
  `−=` (regular sale).
- **Pick failure:** cancel and `available +=`.
- **No-show:** done by staff, conditional on `picked`, then `available +=`.

**Scale:** batch per store first, then shard by store or store group.

## K. Real-world bonus (not scored)

- **Stock accuracy in physical retail** is typically only 60–80% per SKU and location because of
  shrinkage, misplaced items and receiving errors. Retailers run **cycle counts** (staff recount a
  shelf and overwrite `quantity`), which would be another staff action that resets the counter.
- **Safety stock for online availability**: most retailers hide the last N units per store online,
  for exactly the pick-failure reason above.
- **Reserve on the shelf vs reserve in the system**: some chains pick within minutes ("ready in 1
  hour") to shrink the window in which walk-ins can take reserved items.
- **POS integration** is often batch-based (end of day) in older systems. Near-real-time events like
  in this prompt are the modern setup.
