## A. Assessment: PASS, strong — the best mock overall

Three tracked habits fired **without prompts** in the same session:

1. **The dominant user action asked first** ("what are the most common actions?" was your first
   question) and designed first (the seat map).
2. **Query → index for every path**, plus the lock stated while designing, with deadlock avoidance.
3. **Phase 4 opened by you**, with numbers and occupancy on the right resource. That's the first time
   in five mocks.

The core hold → pay → confirm lifecycle ended in a robust shape: conditional hold, lazy expiry,
`paying`, shared payment expiry, status query before releasing. Getting there took two probes on the
late-payment race.

## B. Three strongest things

1. **Phase 4 unprompted**, with the method you learned two mocks ago applied to the right unit.
2. **Concurrency design**: all-or-nothing conditional hold, fixed lock order, expiry evaluated inside
   the hold, so correctness never depends on the background worker.
3. **Self-corrections without probes**: the write removed from the seat-map read, and the provider
   status query added before freeing.

## C. Three biggest risks for the real interview

1. **Schema drift within the same session**: a status you designed 10 minutes earlier (`paying`) was
   missing from the table. Re-read the state machine against the table before presenting it.
2. **Lifecycle races at boundaries**: hold expiry vs payment completion needed probes. For every
   timer, ask "what if the other event arrives one second after the timer fires?"
3. **Don't apply a formula to the wrong unit**: ρ = λ × S is per serialized resource. You fixed it
   immediately; say the unit out loud first.

## D. Architectural gaps or inconsistencies

- `paying` (spoken) vs `free|held|confirmed` (schema).
- `booking_seats(booking_id, seat_id)` without the screening.
- Screening = one start time (stakeholder) vs `date_range` (schema).
- `ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING` vs "the scheduler sends new **and changed** screenings".
- The diagram has no worker → provider edge.

## E. Missing non-functional considerations

- Callback signature verification.
- A product SLI: holds that expire unpaid, payments that land after expiry (refunds).
- Seat-map freshness at premieres (replica lag vs "seat looks free but isn't"). The hold re-checks
  on the primary, so it's harmless, but say so.

## F. Over/under-engineering

- **Right-sized**: one Postgres + replicas (justified at ~280 QPS + bursts), a seat-free worker, a
  consumer, the redirect + callback.
- **Under (minor)**: callback authentication.

## G. What a strong candidate might have covered

- `screening_seats` status `free → held → paying → confirmed` with `booking_id`, `held_until`; `bookings`
  with `screening_id, user_id, amount, status, payment_reference UNIQUE, payment_expires_at`.
- The hold as `SELECT … WHERE screening_id = ? AND seat_id = ANY(?) ORDER BY seat_id FOR UPDATE`, then
  check all N are free or expired, then update; rows ≠ N → abort. That makes the lock order explicit.
- A paid callback after release: if the seats are still free, confirm them anyway; if they're taken,
  auto-refund and notify (the compensation), which `paying` + the provider expiry make rare.

## H. Topics to practise before the next mock

1. **State machine ↔ table check**: every status and timestamp in your flow appears in the schema.
2. **Timer races**: for each expiry, the "event arrives right after the timer" case.
3. Keep doing what worked today: most-common-action first, query → index, Phase 4 opener.

## I. A better architecture (post-mock, brief)

Same boxes. Data: `screening_seats(screening_id, seat_id, status free|held|paying|confirmed,
booking_id, held_until)`, `bookings(booking_id, screening_id, user_id, amount, status,
payment_reference UNIQUE, payment_expires_at)`. Hold = ordered `FOR UPDATE` + all-or-nothing; pay =
`held → paying` + provider session expiring at `held_until`; callback (signed, deduped) → confirm
conditionally, or refund if the seats are gone. The worker releases expired `held` seats directly and
`paying` seats only after a provider status query. Premiere: per-screening occupancy is fine at 10x;
beyond that, a waiting room, or shard by `screening_id`.

## K. Real-world bonus (not scored)

- **Virtual waiting rooms** (e.g. Queue-it) are standard for ticket on-sales: users get a queue
  position before reaching the seat map, which caps the load on the booking system.
- **Holds in Redis with TTL** are common at very large scale (Ticketmaster-like), with the DB as the
  confirmation store.
- **Bots/scalpers**: per-user and per-IP rate limits and CAPTCHA at on-sale time.
- **Seat-selection rules**: many cinemas forbid leaving a single empty seat between bookings, a
  validation inside the hold.
- **Seat-map caching**: a short-TTL cache or CDN snapshot of availability absorbs premiere read
  storms, since the hold re-checks anyway.
